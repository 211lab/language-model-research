# INSTRUCTIONS.md

You begin each morning facing a list that grows faster than you can finish it. Messages and meetings arrive without warning and push the work that actually moves your goals to the bottom. By evening most of the important items remain untouched while small tasks have filled the hours. The lesson makes the tension clear: without a daily cutoff and a strict order, pressure from work spreads in every direction and produces motion instead of completed results. The truth is that only three tasks per day carry the real load. The desired outcome is to name those three first, finish them before 2 p.m., and let everything else wait.

## D – Dump every task onto one list first thing so nothing stays in your head.

Write every open item—work projects, errands, follow-ups, and half-finished requests—onto a single sheet before you decide what matters.  
- Avoid: listing only the tasks already on your mind or grouping items by project before the full dump is complete.  
- Works better: writing each task as a single, concrete line that could stand alone, including items that arrived overnight or were carried from yesterday.

## I – Identify the three tasks that will move your main goals if nothing else gets done.

From the full list, select the three items that would still matter next week if everything else stayed undone. Mark them clearly as the A tasks.  
- Avoid: choosing tasks that feel urgent only because they arrived first or because someone else is waiting for a quick reply.  
- Works better: naming tasks that directly advance one of your stated goals or close a project that has been open for weeks.

## R – Rank the rest as B or C so you know what can wait.

Label every remaining item B if it is important but secondary, or C if it can be postponed or dropped without immediate cost.  
- Avoid: leaving items unranked or marking too many as B because they “might matter later.”  
- Works better: assigning a single letter to each task and noting any that can be delegated or deleted outright.

## E – Execute the top three in order during your highest-energy hours.

Place the three A tasks into the first available high-energy blocks and begin work on them in sequence.  
- Avoid: starting with a B or C item because it looks quicker or because email is already open.  
- Works better: treating the first A task as an appointment that cannot be moved and completing it before any other work begins.

## C – Close the door on new requests until the A tasks are finished.

When a new message or request arrives, record it on the list but do not act on it until the three A tasks are complete.  
- Avoid: answering immediately or allowing the new item to replace one of the original A tasks without explicit review.  
- Works better: replying only after the A tasks are done or asking the requester which current A task should be moved down to make room.

## T – Track what got done at the end of the day and move only the unfinished A tasks forward.

At the close of work, mark each completed task and carry forward only the A tasks that remain unfinished.  
- Avoid: rewriting the entire list or carrying over B and C items without re-ranking them.  
- Works better: noting exactly which A tasks finished, which did not, and the single reason each unfinished A task was delayed.

Finish the worksheet now. Write the concrete answers for each letter, then act on the three A tasks tomorrow morning exactly as you have recorded them.
