# INSTRUCTIONS.md

You end most days tired without proof of progress. Forty emails answered, a few fires put out, one meeting that should have been an email — and the one task that actually mattered slid to tomorrow. That's not a discipline failure. It's a sorting failure. You've been treating every task as equal weight, which means whatever shouts loudest wins, not whatever matters most. This worksheet exists to fix that, one letter at a time, using the TRIAGE model. Work through it in order. Do not skip a letter or combine two into one shortcut. Each one builds the surface — the Anvil — that your effort needs to actually shape something today instead of just wearing you out.

## T – Target Your List

Start by emptying your head completely. Every task on your mind goes onto the page — work tasks, home tasks, errands, that thing you've been avoiding for two weeks, the small thing you'll forget by 10am if you don't write it down now. Do not rank, judge, or organize anything yet. This step has one job: get it all out of your skull and onto paper so nothing is hiding in the background, quietly draining your attention.

- Avoid: writing only the "important-sounding" tasks and leaving out the small annoying ones, because those small ones are exactly what derails you later.
- Avoid: vague entries like "work stuff" or "house things" that hide three tasks inside one line.
- Works better: "Reply to client contract email," "Refill prescription," "Call dentist to reschedule," "Finish slide 4 of the deck" — separate, specific, visible.
- Works better: writing down the thing you're dreading most, by name, even if it makes you wince.

## R – Rank By Impact

Now look at the full dump and ask the one honest question: if you only finished three things today, which three would actually move you forward? Not which ones are loudest. Not which ones are easiest to check off. Which ones matter. Mark exactly three as your top priority. Everything else gets labeled second-tier or minor — no ties, no "everything is important."

- Avoid: picking three tasks because they're quick wins that will feel good to cross off.
- Avoid: letting an "urgent" label talk you into ranking something high just because it pinged you.
- Works better: picking the task that unblocks other work, protects income, or prevents a bigger problem later — even if it's uncomfortable.
- Works better: writing next to each top-3 task exactly why it made the cut ("moves the project deadline," "fixes a client issue before it escalates").

## I – Isolate What to Skip

Go through everything that didn't make your top 3 and make a real decision about each item: postpone, delegate, or delete. This is not a holding pen for guilt — it's a filter. A shorter, honest list beats a long one you feel bad about every time you glance at it.

- Avoid: leaving everything else as "I'll get to it later" with no actual decision made.
- Avoid: keeping a task on the list out of guilt when it hasn't mattered in weeks.
- Works better: marking specific tasks "Delegate to [name]" or "Delete — hasn't mattered in 3 weeks" or "Postpone to Thursday."
- Works better: being honest that a task you keep avoiding might not deserve a spot on the list at all.

## A – Allocate Prime Time

Take your top 3 tasks and assign them to your highest-energy hours of the day, treating each block like an appointment you cannot cancel. Write the actual time on the worksheet — not "morning," but "8:30–9:30." Then slot your second-tier and minor tasks into your lower-energy hours, batching similar small ones together instead of scattering them through the day.

- Avoid: writing "sometime today" next to a top-3 task — that's how it gets bumped.
- Avoid: scheduling your hardest task for the hour you're usually the most drained.
- Works better: "9:00–9:25 — draft proposal (Pomodoro sprint), 9:25–9:30 break."
- Works better: batching all your calls and quick errands into one 30-minute block instead of letting them interrupt three different parts of your day.

## G – Guard Your Focus

Write down exactly how you'll protect the time block you just scheduled. This means naming the specific distractions you'll remove before you start — not a general intention to "focus better." Use short sprints: 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes off, repeat. Note who you'll tell you're unavailable and for how long.

- Avoid: "I'll try to stay focused" — that's not a plan, it's a hope.
- Avoid: leaving your phone in the same room, face up, "just in case."
- Works better: "Phone in the drawer, Slack status set to Focus Mode 9–10am, tabs closed except the doc I'm working in."
- Works better: writing the sprint structure directly on the worksheet: "25 min work / 5 min break x2 to finish this task."

## E – Evaluate and Reset

At the end of the day, come back to the worksheet and check off what actually got done. Be honest about what didn't. Carry unfinished tasks forward to tomorrow's list, and adjust their priority if needed — a task that got bumped once might deserve top-3 status tomorrow. If something has been skipped three days running, make a real call: cut it, or break it into a smaller, more doable piece.

- Avoid: skipping this step because the day already feels over and you're tired.
- Avoid: silently re-adding a dodged task without asking why it keeps getting dodged.
- Works better: "Task not done — carrying forward, moving to top 3 tomorrow since it's now overdue."
- Works better: "Skipped 3 days straight — cutting it, it was never a real priority" or "Skipped 3 days — breaking into a 15-minute first step for tomorrow."

Finish every letter before you call the worksheet done — a half-built Anvil still wobbles. Once T through E are filled in with real, specific answers, you have a sorted day instead of a loose pile of tasks. Do this tomorrow morning before you touch a single task, and prove by sundown that you know the difference between busy and built.
