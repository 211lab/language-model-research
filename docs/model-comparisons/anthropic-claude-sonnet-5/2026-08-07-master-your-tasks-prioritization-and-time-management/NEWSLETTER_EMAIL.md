## Subject
Your List Isn't the Problem. What You're Missing Is the Anvil.

## Body

You already know the feeling this week is built around: forty emails answered, a few fires put out, a meeting that should've been a text, and somehow the one task that actually mattered slid to tomorrow again. That's not a discipline problem. It's a sorting problem, and this week's packet exists to fix exactly that.

This week's essay uses an old anvil sitting in a neighbor's garage to make a simple point: the hammer isn't what's missing from your days, the fire isn't what's missing either. You've got plenty of both. What's missing is a fixed surface to strike against. Without it, every task you touch just slides — you swing all day, hit forty things, and shape nothing. Urgent noise wins over important work not because it deserves to, but because it made a sound and important work didn't. The essay walks through why that's a sorting failure, not a motivation failure, and introduces the daily practice — TRIAGE — that builds the missing surface fresh every morning.

That surface is what this week's worksheet actually builds. It's the centerpiece of everything else in the packet, and it's where the real work of the week happens. The lesson explains the problem and the model. The worksheet is where you stop reading about sorting and actually do it, one letter at a time: dump everything in your head onto the page, rank it down to a real top three with reasons attached, make honest calls on everything else, block your best hours like appointments you can't cancel, name your actual distraction-removal plan, and close the day by checking what got shaped and resetting for tomorrow.

Don't skim it. Don't combine steps to save time. Each letter is load-bearing — a half-finished Anvil still wobbles, and a wobbling Anvil barely beats no Anvil at all. Fill in real, specific answers: actual task names, actual clock times, actual named distractions removed. Vague entries like "work stuff" or "try to focus" will just put you right back to swinging at loose metal tomorrow morning.

This worksheet is where this week's transformation actually happens — not in the reading, in the doing. It's the daily practice that turns a growing, guilt-inducing list into a shorter, honest one, and turns scattered effort into visible proof of progress by sundown. That proof is what carries forward into next week's system-building, because a sorted day repeated five times straight is what starts to feel like a system you can trust instead of a list you're dodging.

Read this week's full context essay for the anvil story and the reasoning behind why sorting beats trying harder — it'll make the worksheet click faster once you sit down with it.

And don't run the worksheet cold. Bring it into the GPT coach and work T through E in order, one letter per exchange. It will push back on vague answers, demand real task names and real clock times, and won't let you skip ahead until each letter is actually built — which is the whole point, since a half-built Anvil still wobbles.

Nobody sorts your list for you. Nobody guards your best hour but you. Pick your three tomorrow morning, protect the first block like it's non-negotiable, and prove by sundown that you know the difference between busy and built.
