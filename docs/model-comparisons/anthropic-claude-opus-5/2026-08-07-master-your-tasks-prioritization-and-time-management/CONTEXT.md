# CONTEXT.md

## Title
Finish the Three Tasks That Actually Move Your Life Forward — Before Lunch

## Subtitle
Your list isn't too long. It's unsorted. Learn the 60-second sort that tells you what to do first, what to push, and what to cut — so the loud stuff stops stealing your best hours.

## Promise
In 7 days, you will complete your 3 highest-impact tasks before 12:00 p.m. on at least 5 of those days — without adding work hours, without buying a new app, and without cutting family time.

## Motivations
- Walk out of the workday with the important project moved forward, not just the inbox emptied.
- Know at 6:00 a.m. exactly what the day's three wins are, in order.
- Stop carrying the same unfinished task from Monday to Friday to next Monday.
- Say "no" or "not today" to a request without a two-day guilt hangover.
- Have real proof of progress at week's end: goals advanced, not just chores survived.

## Challenges & Sacrifices
- A boss, client, or coworker drops "urgent" work on you at 9:15 a.m., and it never looks optional.
- Kids' schedules, school pickups, and bedtime routines lock up the hours you'd rather use for deep work.
- Tasks live in four places: your phone notes, a work ticket system, a sticky note, and your head.
- Your highest-energy hours get spent on messages, meetings, and small requests from other people.
- Choosing your top three means openly admitting that other things won't get done today — and telling someone so.

## Frustrations
- You were busy for ten straight hours and can't name one thing that mattered.
- The big project keeps sliding a week, then a month, then a quarter.
- You start a task, get pinged, and come back 40 minutes later having lost your place.
- Your to-do list is 37 items long and every one of them feels equally loud.
- You end the day tired but behind, so you work at night and still wake up behind.

## Transformations
- From "busy with nothing to show" to naming three completed wins at dinner.
- From a project that slips every week to visible progress logged every single day.
- From losing 40 minutes to one ping to finishing a 25-minute focus sprint uninterrupted.
- From 37 equally loud items to a short A-list, a B-list, and a cut list.
- From working nights to catch up to closing the laptop with the day's priorities done.

## Choice
You can keep running your day off whatever shouts loudest — answering every ping, calling the day "productive" because it was full, and watching the work that actually matters get pushed to a "someday" that never lands on the calendar. That path works fine for everyone else's priorities. Or you can spend 10 minutes sorting before the day starts, protect two focus blocks, and end this week able to point at three finished things that moved you forward — not because you found more hours, but because you stopped spending your best ones on the wrong tasks.

## Actionable VERB
TRIAGE

## Model

**T — Tally everything.**
You cannot rank a list you can't see. Tonight, or first thing in the morning, take a blank page and dump every task in your head for the next 7 days: work projects, the leaking faucet, the call to your dad, the form for school, the gym. Don't sort, don't judge, just empty it out. Include the ones you feel guilty about. Then check your other storage spots — phone notes, email flags, work tickets, sticky notes — and add anything hiding there. Aim for one list in one place. Most men land between 20 and 50 items. That number isn't a problem; it's the first honest picture you've had in months.

**R — Rank by impact, not by noise.**
Go down the list and ask one question per item: "If I only got three things done today, which three would move my goals or duties the farthest?" Mark those three with an **A**. Only three. Mark clearly important but non-urgent work as **B**. Mark small errands and quick chores as **C**. Then run the urgent/important check on anything that feels loud: urgent *and* important is an A; important but not urgent is a B and gets scheduled, not ignored; urgent but not important is a C and often belongs to somebody else's agenda. If more than three items scream "A," you're describing a week, not a day — pick today's three and date the rest.

**I — Isolate what you won't do.**
Prioritizing isn't a list of yeses; it's a list of noes. Go through the leftovers and mark each one: **Delay** (give it a real date), **Delegate** (write the name of the person and send the handoff today), or **Delete** (say it out loud: this is not happening, and I'm okay with that). Anything that has ridden on your list for three weeks untouched is either not important — cut it — or too big — break it into a first 20-minute step. Expect one uncomfortable conversation here. Send the short message: "I can't get to this by Thursday. I can do it Monday, or Dana can take it now." That sentence buys back your morning.

**A — Assign prime time to the A-list.**
Open your schedule and put your A-tasks in your two highest-energy hours — for most men that's early, before the world starts making requests. Block them like a doctor's appointment with a name attached: not "work," but "draft the client proposal — section 1." Give A-1 a 60- to 90-minute block, then A-2, then A-3. Push B tasks to the low-energy dip in the afternoon. Batch your C tasks: all calls in one 30-minute run, all errands in one loop, all messages in two checks a day instead of forty. If your morning truly belongs to someone else, claim a different fixed window and defend it just as hard.

**G — Guard the block.**
A protected hour beats a scattered five. Before the block starts: phone on silent and face down in a drawer, notifications off, unrelated tabs closed, and a heads-up to whoever might interrupt — "I'm heads-down until 10:30, back to you after." Then run focus sprints: 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes up and moving, repeat. When a new thought or request shows up mid-sprint, don't chase it — write it on a capture pad and keep going. That pad is where interruptions go to wait. Work until the task is done or the block is up. When you finish an A-task, check it off by hand. That mark is your scoreboard.

**E — Evaluate and reset for tomorrow.**
Take five minutes at day's end. Answer three things in writing: Which of my three A-tasks got finished? What actually pulled me off track? What's tomorrow's three? Roll unfinished A-tasks forward, but re-rank them — don't assume yesterday's answer still holds. Watch for repeat offenders: the same task surviving three days means it's either mislabeled (demote or delete it) or too vague (cut the first small step out of it). Do this loop every night for seven nights, then look back at the week. You'll have a written record of 15 to 21 finished priorities — proof that the list now serves you instead of running you.

## Action Steps Summary

**1. Dump It, Then Rank It**
Empty every task out of your head and your four storage spots onto one page, then mark exactly three A-tasks using the impact question and the urgent/important check. Outcome: you walk into the day knowing your first move instead of scanning for the loudest fire.

**2. Cut, Hand Off, and Block**
Name what you're delaying, delegating, or deleting — including one conversation you've been avoiding — then put your three A-tasks in your highest-energy hours and batch the small stuff. Outcome: your best hours finally hold your most important work.

**3. Guard and Score It**
Silence the phone, run 25-minute sprints, park interruptions on a capture pad, check off finished tasks by hand, and spend five minutes each night re-ranking tomorrow's three. Outcome: a week of written proof and a list that shrinks instead of growing.

## FAQ

**Why am I working ten-hour days and still falling behind?**
Because hours aren't the problem — order is. Most of the day is going to tasks that feel urgent but don't move anything forward. Rank three high-impact tasks each morning and do them first, and the same ten hours start producing visible progress.

**Why do I feel guilty relaxing at night even when I worked all day?**
Because nothing important got closed. Unfinished priorities keep running in the background. Finishing three ranked tasks before lunch gives you a clear stopping point, so the evening actually feels earned.

**How do I get people to stop dumping things on me?**
You don't wait for them to stop — you answer with a date instead of a yes. "I can start that Monday" or "Dana can take it now" is a full answer. The delay-delegate-delete pass in this model gives you those words before you need them.

**What if my job means real emergencies interrupt everything?**
Then protect a smaller window earlier. Ninety minutes at 6:30 a.m. beats a wide-open afternoon that gets shredded by 9:15. Emergencies get the rest of the day; your top task gets the first block.

**I've tried planners and apps and quit every time. Why would this stick?**
Because the failure wasn't the tool, it was the missing sort. A list of 37 items with no A/B/C ranking feels the same whether it's on paper or in an app. This week you rank, cut, and review — five minutes a night, no new software required.
