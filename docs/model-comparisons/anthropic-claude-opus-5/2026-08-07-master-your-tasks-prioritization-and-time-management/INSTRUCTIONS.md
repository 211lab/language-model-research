## Master Your Tasks: Prioritization and Time Management

You worked a full day yesterday. Name one thing that mattered.

If the answer comes slowly, you already know why you're here. Most men can name activity — messages cleared, meetings attended, a queue knocked down, a call returned to someone who didn't need it. What they can't name is movement. The project that decides whether next year looks different slid again, quietly, the same way it slid last week and the week before, because nothing that matters ever yells.

That's the tension this worksheet exists to break. The important work is quiet. The unimportant work is loud. Run your day off volume and you will spend your best hours serving other people's priorities while calling it a full day.

Here's the truth underneath it: **your list isn't too long — it's unsorted.** Thirty-seven items is normal. Fifty is normal. The problem is that all of them sit at the same volume in your head, so every time you look, you scan for whatever feels most on fire instead of whatever moves you farthest. An unsorted list is not a plan. It's noise with your name on it. You do not need more hours. You need a sort.

And a real sort has a cost. Prioritizing is not a list of yeses — it's a list of noes. Three A-tasks means the rest get delayed, handed off, or killed, and at least one person has to hear about it. That uncomfortable sentence is the price of your morning. Most men would rather stay busy forever than say it.

The outcome you're after by the end of this worksheet is narrow and verifiable: three high-impact tasks finished before 12:00 p.m. on at least five days out of seven — no new hours, no new app, no cutting family time. To get there, the worksheet walks the **TRIAGE** model one letter at a time. Write everything down. In your head, these answers will lie to you.

---

## T — Tally Everything

You cannot rank a list you can't see. This section is a full dump — every task in your head for the next seven days, with no sorting and no judging. Work projects, the leaking faucet, the call to your dad, the school form, the gym. Include the ones you feel guilty about; those especially, because guilt is what keeps a task hidden and heavy at the same time.

Fill the tally section by emptying your head first, then raiding your other storage spots. Go to your phone notes. Go to your flagged emails. Go to the work ticket system. Go to the sticky notes on the monitor and the one in your truck. Everything lands on this one page, in this one place. If you leave a task in a second location, your brain will keep running background processes to guard it.

Most men land between 20 and 50 items. Don't stop until the page looks uglier than you expected. That number isn't a failure — it's the first honest picture of your life in months.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- "Work stuff" or "house things" — categories, not tasks
- Stopping at ten items because the page started to look bad
- Skipping the item you've been avoiding for a month because writing it down makes it real
- Leaving half the list in your phone and telling yourself you'll remember it
- Pre-sorting as you write, or deciding an item is "not worth listing"

**What works better:**
- "Draft client proposal — section 1" as its own line, separate from "email Dana the revised budget"
- Sweeping all four storage spots in one pass and adding whatever's hiding there
- Writing "call Dad — haven't in three weeks" even though it stings
- Listing the leaking faucet, the school form, and the gym alongside work — one life, one list
- Letting the count run to 40 without commentary

## R — Rank by Impact, Not by Noise

Now you sort, and there is only one question per item: *"If I only got three things done today, which three would move my goals or duties the farthest?"* Mark those three with an **A**. Only three. Clearly important but non-urgent work gets a **B**. Small errands and quick chores get a **C**.

Then run the urgent/important check on anything that feels loud. Urgent *and* important is an A. Important but not urgent is a B — and it gets a date written next to it, not a shrug. Urgent but not important is a C, and it usually belongs to somebody else's agenda; label it that way and stop pretending otherwise.

If more than three items scream A, you're describing a week, not a day. Pick today's three and put dates on the rest. The whole value of this section is the constraint. Four A's is the same as zero A's, because a list of four is a list you'll scan again tomorrow morning looking for the loudest one.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Marking six or seven items A because "they're all important"
- Ranking by deadline pressure instead of impact
- Giving A status to something because a coworker's tone made it feel hot
- Writing B next to something and leaving the date blank
- Choosing the three easiest items so the day feels winnable

**What works better:**
- Exactly three A's, numbered A-1, A-2, A-3 in order of impact
- "Urgent but not important — this is Marcus's priority, not mine" written next to the loud item
- "B — Tuesday 2:00 p.m." instead of "B — soon"
- Picking the A that scares you slightly because it's the one that actually moves the project
- Crossing out a fourth A and writing a date on it in the same motion

## I — Isolate What You Won't Do

Every leftover on the page gets one of three marks: **Delay**, **Delegate**, or **Delete**. No item escapes unmarked. An unmarked leftover is a task you've silently agreed to carry around all week.

Delay requires a real date — a day and, where it matters, a time. Delegate requires a name, and the handoff message goes out today, not eventually. Delete requires you to say it out loud: *this is not happening, and I'm okay with that.* Say the sentence. The relief is the point.

Then handle the fossils. Anything that has ridden your list for three weeks untouched is either not important — cut it — or too big, in which case you break out a first 20-minute step and write that step as the task.

Expect one uncomfortable message here, and write it in the worksheet before you send it. Keep it short. No apology paragraph. A date or a name: *"I can't get to this by Thursday. I can do it Monday, or Dana can take it now."* That one sentence buys back your morning. Skipping it is how you end up back where you started with a nicer-looking list.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- "Delay — later" or "Delay — next week sometime"
- "Delegate — someone on the team"
- Marking Delete but leaving the item on your mental list "just in case"
- Drafting a three-paragraph apology that invites negotiation
- Leaving the uncomfortable message unsent and calling the section done

**What works better:**
- "Delay — Thursday 9/11, 3:00 p.m."
- "Delegate — Dana. Handoff sent 8:40 p.m. tonight."
- "Delete — the conference proposal. Not happening. I'm okay with that."
- "Been on the list 5 weeks → too big. First step: outline three headings, 20 min."
- One short message written verbatim in the worksheet, then sent tonight

## A — Assign Prime Time to the A-List

A ranking that never reaches a calendar is an opinion. This section turns your three A's into named blocks with start times.

Put A-1 in your two highest-energy hours — for most men that's early, before the world starts making requests. Give it 60 to 90 minutes. Then A-2, then A-3. Block them like a doctor's appointment, with a name attached: not "work on the proposal," but "Draft client proposal — section 1, 6:30 to 8:00." A vague block is a puddle; a named block with a start time is a channel.

Push B tasks into the afternoon dip, where lower energy is fine. Batch the C's: all calls in one 30-minute run, all errands in one loop, all message checks down to two a day instead of forty. Write the batch windows into the worksheet as blocks too — unbatched small tasks will leak into your A-block and drain it.

If your mornings genuinely belong to someone else — a shift, a crew, a kid's bus schedule — claim a different fixed window and defend it just as hard. The requirement isn't sunrise. It's a fixed, named, defended slot.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- "Morning — proposal"
- Leaving the block undated or unscheduled and trusting yourself to "fit it in"
- Scheduling A-1 for 9:30 a.m., after the requests start arriving
- Stacking all three A's back-to-back with no breathing room
- Leaving C tasks scattered across the day as they arrive

**What works better:**
- "A-1: Draft client proposal — section 1 — 6:30–8:00 a.m."
- "A-2: Fix the pricing sheet formulas — 8:15–9:00 a.m."
- "C batch: all calls — 1:30–2:00 p.m. All errands — one loop after pickup."
- "Messages: 11:30 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. only."
- "Mornings are the crew's. My window: 8:00–9:30 p.m., Mon–Fri, defended."

## G — Guard the Block

A protected hour beats a scattered five. This section is your pre-block checklist and your interruption protocol, written down before you need it — because mid-sprint you will not have the discipline to invent it.

Write your setup steps as commitments: phone on silent and face down in a drawer, notifications off, unrelated tabs closed, and a heads-up to whoever might interrupt — *"I'm heads-down until 10:30, back to you after."* Name the person you need to tell and write the exact sentence you'll say to them.

Then run focus sprints: 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes up and moving, repeat. When a new thought or request arrives mid-sprint, don't chase it. Write it on the capture pad and keep going. Designate that pad in the worksheet — a specific pad, a specific place on the desk. That pad is where interruptions go to wait.

Work until the task is done or the block is up. When you finish an A-task, check it off by hand. Not in an app. By hand. That mark is your scoreboard, and the physical act is part of what makes the next day possible.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- "Try to stay focused" or "avoid distractions"
- Keeping the phone on the desk face-up "in case something important comes in"
- Deciding to "just quickly check" a ping mid-sprint
- Working straight through 90 minutes with no break and burning out by day three
- Leaving the interruption capture spot undefined

**What works better:**
- "Phone: silent, in the kitchen drawer, requires standing up to reach."
- "Heads-up to Marcus at 6:25: 'Heads-down until 8:00, back to you after.'"
- "Sprints: 6:30–6:55, up 5. 7:00–7:25, up 5. 7:30–8:00."
- "Capture pad: yellow legal pad, top right of desk. Everything mid-sprint goes there."
- "Check off A-1 by hand on the page the moment it's done."

## E — Evaluate and Reset for Tomorrow

Five minutes at day's end, in writing. Three questions, answered honestly: Which of my three A-tasks got finished? What actually pulled me off track? What are tomorrow's three?

Roll unfinished A-tasks forward, but re-rank them. Don't assume yesterday's answer still holds — a task that was A-1 this morning may be a B by tonight, and pretending otherwise is how a stale item rides your list for a month.

Watch for repeat offenders. The same task surviving three days means one of two things: it's mislabeled, so demote or delete it, or it's too vague, so cut the first small step out of it and write that step instead. Log it in the worksheet either way. Vagueness disguised as importance is the most expensive item on your list.

Run this loop for seven nights. Then read all seven entries in one sitting and count the finished A-tasks. You should have a written record of 15 to 21 completed priorities. That's not a feeling of progress. That's evidence — and it's the number you're worth per week when your heat has a channel.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- "Decent day, got some stuff done"
- "I got distracted" without naming what and when
- Copying tomorrow's three straight from today without re-ranking
- Letting a three-day survivor slide a fourth day untouched
- Doing the review in your head on the drive home

**What works better:**
- "A-1 finished. A-2 finished. A-3 not started."
- "Pulled off at 9:15 by Marcus's 'urgent' spreadsheet — urgent, not important. Should have gone to the capture pad."
- "A-3 rolls to tomorrow but drops to A-2; new A-1 is the proposal review."
- "The website copy has now survived 3 days. Too vague → new task: 'write homepage headline, 20 min.'"
- "Was the cost of saying 'not today' as big as I feared? No. Marcus said 'no problem.'"

## Finish It Tonight

Tally, rank, isolate, assign, guard, evaluate. Insight in one end, finished work out the other, same day.

Don't leave this worksheet half-filled with the calendar section blank. That's the section that decides everything. A page of rankings with no named block at a fixed time is an opinion, and opinions don't move projects. Write the three A's. Mark every leftover with a date, a name, or a deletion. Send the one uncomfortable message before you sleep. Put A-1 in your calendar with a name and a start time. Set the phone to silent and put it somewhere that requires standing up.

Tomorrow morning, don't open your inbox first. Open this page. Start A-1 at the time you wrote. Twenty-five on, five up. Interruptions go on the pad. Check it off by hand.

You already know what your three tasks are. You've known for months. Cut the groove tonight — or admit you'd rather stay busy than get anywhere.
