## Post

There's a sticky note on the side of my monitor. Seven words. A project I meant to start in March. The ink has faded where the sun hits it, and I've peeled it off to wipe the desk and stuck it right back, which tells you more than the note does.

Every day I sat two feet from that note and did other work instead. Not lazy work — real work. Messages answered. Meetings attended. Ten-hour days, some of them. Then the laptop again at 9:30 at night, tired and dull, getting forty minutes of bad work done before giving up.

The question that finally got under my skin: you worked ten hours yesterday. Name one thing that mattered.

Most of us can name activity. Very few of us can name a result.

Here's what I've come to believe about that gap. A full day and a useful day feel identical from the inside. Effort feels like progress. Fatigue feels like proof. And the reason the important work keeps sliding isn't discipline — it's that the important work is quiet and the unimportant work is loud. The project that decides whether next year looks different doesn't ping you. It doesn't stand in your doorway. Somebody else's forgotten planning arrives with a siren attached and instructions already built in.

Feeling loses to instruction every time.

That's this week. Not more hours, not more drive — a sort. Which is why the worksheet is the thing I want you working from before we get in a room together.

The worksheet is where the sort actually happens on paper. Every task out of your head and out of your four hiding places onto one page. Three A-tasks named, not four. The leftovers marked as delay with a date, delegate with a name, or delete out loud. Your first A-task written into your two best hours with a start time attached. It's a groove cut in the floor between "I know what matters" and "I did what matters before noon."

The lesson and the lecture exist to help you fill it out honestly. They'll explain why your thirty-seven items all feel equally loud, why the urgent stuff wins by default, and what it costs to say not today. But none of that becomes anything until it's written down somewhere with your handwriting on it.

Do the worksheet before the discussion. Not after. The conversation we're going to have is about what happened when you tried it — the item you couldn't bring yourself to delete, the person you had to send the uncomfortable message to, the morning that got hijacked at 9:15 anyway. That's the interesting part, and it only exists if you ran the thing first.

I'll say this now so nobody's surprised: the hardest line on the page isn't the A-list. It's the noes. Three top tasks means thirty-four things get delayed, handed off, or killed, and at least one person has to hear about it. Most men would rather stay busy forever than say one uncomfortable sentence. We're going to talk about that sentence.

Bring your page. Bring what broke. Come ready to say out loud what's been on your monitor since March.
