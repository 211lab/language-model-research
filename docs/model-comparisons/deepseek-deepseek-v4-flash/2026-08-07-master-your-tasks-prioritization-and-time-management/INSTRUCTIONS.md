# INSTRUCTIONS.md

You wake up with good intentions. You have a list of things you want to do. By noon, you’ve answered fifteen emails, put out two fires for other people, and somehow the one project that would actually move your career forward hasn’t been touched. You end the day exhausted, wondering where the time went, and feeling guilty that you’re busy but not effective.

This is not a time problem. You have enough hours. This is a prioritization problem disguised as a busy schedule. You are reacting to whatever screams loudest instead of choosing what matters most. The truth is simpler than you think. You don’t need more discipline. You need a system that forces the right tasks to the top and keeps the noise out. A system that turns your chaotic to-do list into a clear, executable plan.

That system is called FOCUS. It works because it doesn’t ask you to be perfect. It asks you to be intentional. The worksheet you are about to complete will walk you through each letter of the FOCUS method, one step at a time. By the end, you will have a concrete, prioritized plan for your day and a repeatable process to take control of your time.

Work through each section below in order. Be honest. Be specific. The goal is not to fill out a perfect worksheet – it is to produce a plan you will actually follow.

---

## F – Filter your full list

Start by dumping every task, errand, and idea that is currently in your head onto the worksheet. Do not judge. Do not filter. Just capture everything – work projects, personal errands, emails you need to send, calls to return, ideas you want to explore. Write them all down.

Once your list is complete, filter ruthlessly. Ask yourself: “If I could only do three things today, which three would create the biggest positive impact?” Circle those three. Label them as your A-tasks. Everything else is either a B-task (important but not urgent), a C-task (minor errand), or a candidate for deletion or delegation.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Listing more than three A-tasks because everything feels urgent
- Keeping tasks that are vague, like “work on project” instead of “draft the proposal outline”
- Including tasks that belong to someone else or that you could delegate

**What works better in your answer:**
- Choosing tasks that directly move you toward a key goal or fulfill a critical duty
- Writing each A-task as a specific, actionable outcome (e.g., “Complete the budget review for Q3”)
- Being willing to let go of tasks that feel busy but produce no real progress

---

## O – Order by impact, not urgency

Take your three A-tasks and rank them by long-term value. Do not rank them by how loud they scream – ignore the false urgency of deadlines, emails, notifications, and other people’s emergencies. The most important task might not be due tomorrow, but it is the one that moves you toward your goals. Put that task first. Your order determines your outcome.

Write your three A-tasks in order of impact on the worksheet. Task 1 is the one you will work on first. Task 2 is second. Task 3 is third.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Ordering by deadline or due date instead of by long-term value
- Putting the easiest or fastest task first just to get a quick win
- Letting a loud, urgent request from someone else bump your highest-impact task to second or third

**What works better in your answer:**
- Asking “Which of these three, if completed, would make everything else easier or less important?”
- Choosing the task that creates the most forward momentum for your week or month
- Trusting your own judgment about what matters, even if no one is demanding it

---

## C – Carve out protected time

For each A-task, block a specific time slot in your day. Ideally, schedule your highest-impact task during your peak energy hours – the time of day when you are most focused and alert. Treat these blocks as non-negotiable appointments with yourself. Close your door, silence your phone, turn off notifications. If you use the Pomodoro technique, commit to at least two cycles (25 minutes work, 5 minutes break) per block.

On the worksheet, write the exact start and end time for each A-task block. Be realistic about how long each task will take. If you are unsure, give yourself more time than you think you need.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Leaving the time slot vague, like “morning” or “after lunch”
- Scheduling your most important task during your lowest energy period
- Forgetting to account for transitions, breaks, or interruptions

**What works better in your answer:**
- Writing a precise time range, such as “9:00 AM – 10:30 AM”
- Matching the task to your energy pattern – creative work in your peak hours, administrative work in your low-energy slots
- Building in a 5-minute buffer between blocks to reset

---

## U – Undertake one task at a time

During your protected block, work on only that one task. No switching to email, no checking Slack, no “quick” side errands. Multitasking is a myth. Single-tasking is the fastest path to completion. If your mind wanders, gently bring it back. You will finish faster and with higher quality than if you tried to juggle three things.

On the worksheet, commit to the single task you will focus on during each block. Write down one specific action you will take if you get stuck or distracted – for example, “take three deep breaths and return to the task” or “write down the distraction on a notepad and deal with it later.”

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Listing multiple tasks for the same block
- Planning to “check email quickly” during your focus time
- Leaving the block open to whatever feels right in the moment

**What works better in your answer:**
- Naming exactly one task per block
- Writing a simple recovery action for when your focus breaks
- Reminding yourself that switching tasks costs you 15-20 minutes of lost momentum each time

---

## S – Savor completion and review

When you finish an A-task, pause. Check it off physically – the act gives a dopamine boost that builds momentum. Take a moment to acknowledge what you accomplished. Then, at the end of the day, review: What got done? What didn’t? Carry unfinished A-tasks to tomorrow’s list, but ask honestly: Is this still a priority? If it keeps slipping, break it into smaller steps or cut it.

On the worksheet, mark each A-task as completed, carried over, or cut. For any task you carry over, write one specific reason it was not finished and one adjustment you will make tomorrow to ensure it gets done. For any task you cut, write why it no longer deserves your attention.

**What to avoid in your answer:**
- Skipping the review because you are tired or in a hurry
- Carrying over the same unfinished task for days without questioning its importance
- Feeling guilty about unfinished tasks instead of learning from the pattern

**What works better in your answer:**
- Checking off completed tasks with a physical pen or a deliberate click
- Writing a concrete reason for each carry-over, such as “underestimated time needed” or “got pulled into a meeting”
- Cutting tasks that have been slipping for three or more days – they are either not important or need to be broken into smaller steps

---

You now have a complete FOCUS plan for your day. The worksheet is not the end – it is the beginning. Take the plan and execute it. Protect your blocks. Work one task at a time. Review at the end of the day. Repeat tomorrow.

The system is here. The method is clear. The only thing missing is your decision to act. Decide now. Not later. Now.
