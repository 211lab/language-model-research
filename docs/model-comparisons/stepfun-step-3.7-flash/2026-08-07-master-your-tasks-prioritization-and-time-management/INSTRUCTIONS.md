# INSTRUCTIONS: Master Your Tasks: Prioritization and Time Management Worksheet

You know the scene: you sit down at your desk at 9 a.m., coffee steaming, ready to crush the day. Your to-do list has 17 items. You check email first to “get it out of the way,” a coworker derails you with a 15-minute tangent, a calendar reminder pops up for an optional meeting you forgot about. By 4 p.m., you’ve been busy for seven hours straight, but the big project you needed to finish to hit your quarterly goal is untouched, and the personal errand you’ve been putting off for a month is still on the list. You leave work exhausted, brain fried, and know you’ll be staying up until 9 p.m. to catch up, again.

This is not a time management problem. It is a structure problem. Unstructured effort is not effort at all—it is noise. You cannot shape raw, unprioritized tasks into meaningful results any more than you can hammer hot metal into a sword on a pile of loose gravel. You need a stable surface to work on. The CONQUER method is how you build that stable surface, called the Anvil, before you lift a finger to do any actual work. This worksheet will walk you through each step of CONQUER to build your Anvil for tomorrow, so you stop spinning your wheels and start shaping real results.

Follow each step below in order, filling out the corresponding section of your worksheet as you go. Be specific, honest, and practical with your answers—generic responses won’t help you build a structure that works for your actual life.

## C – Capture
This step is the foundation of your Anvil: you can’t prioritize tasks you’ve forgotten exist. Your goal here is to get every single task out of your head and onto your list, no sorting, no judgment.
- What to avoid in your answer: Skipping tiny errands like “pick up dry cleaning” or “reply to mom’s text” because you think they’re not important enough to include; only listing work tasks and forgetting personal to-dos; starting to sort or prioritize tasks while you’re brain dumping.
- What works better: Writing down every single item, from “submit quarterly report” to “buy toothpaste” to “call plumber about leaky sink”; keeping the list raw and unsorted during this step, no filtering for what “counts” as a task; if a task pops into your head mid-dump, add it immediately even if it seems random.

## O – Organize
Your raw brain dump is a mess of urgent requests, long-term projects, and tiny errands. This step sorts that mess into tiers so you know exactly what’s worth your time.
- What to avoid in your answer: Putting more than 3 tasks in A-level just because they feel important; labeling a task B-level just because you don’t want to do it; skipping C-level tasks entirely and pretending they don’t exist.
- What works better: A-level is only the 2-3 tasks that, if you complete them tomorrow, make the day a win no matter what else happens (e.g., “finish client proposal draft,” “submit college application essay”); B-level is important but not urgent, or second-tier work that can wait (e.g., “schedule dentist appointment,” “review team meeting notes”); C-level is minor errands or low-priority busywork that can be delegated, postponed, or cut if you run out of time (e.g., “answer non-urgent Slack messages,” “organize desk drawer”).

## N – Narrow
Trying to tackle 10+ tasks a day leaves you jumping between work and finishing nothing important. This step cuts the noise so your effort goes only to what moves the needle.
- What to avoid in your answer: Adding 4 or 5 A-level tasks because you’re worried you’ll forget something; moving on to B or C tasks before your A’s are done just because they feel easier; adding a new request to your A list without checking if it’s more important than your existing A tasks.
- What works better: If you have more than 3 A-level candidates, pick the 2-3 that align most directly with your biggest current goals (work or personal); write your A-level tasks at the very top of your list in big, bold letters, no other tasks above them; if a new request pops up tomorrow, ask “Is this more important than the 2-3 A tasks I planned to hit my goals?” before adding it anywhere.

## Q – Queue
Knowing your top tasks isn’t enough if you waste your highest-energy hours on low-value work or spend the morning deciding what to do next. This step places your prioritized tasks on the stable schedule of your Anvil.
- What to avoid in your answer: Scheduling A-level tasks for mid-afternoon when your energy is low; leaving your A tasks unscheduled and “fitting them in” whenever you have free time; spreading B and C tasks out all day so you’re constantly switching between task types.
- What works better: Blocking your first A task for your highest-energy window (usually first thing in the morning, e.g., 8–9 a.m.), your second A task for the next highest-energy window (e.g., 9–10 a.m.), before you check email or take any requests; batching all B/C tasks into lower-energy times later in the day, or grouping similar tasks (all phone calls, all emails) into a single 30-minute block; if a non-emergency meeting conflicts with your A block, decline or reschedule it.

## U – Unplug
Distractions are the biggest thief of your focused work time. This step clears the space around your Anvil so every strike of your effort lands where you intend.
- What to avoid in your answer: Leaving your phone on silent but still within reach so you check it every time you get stuck; keeping 10+ browser tabs open “just in case” you need them; not telling your team you’re in focus mode so they interrupt you mid-task.
- What works better: Silencing your phone and putting it in a drawer or another room for the full 25–60 minute A-level work block; closing all browser tabs and apps that aren’t directly related to your current A task; putting on noise-canceling headphones and sending a quick message to your team letting them know you’re in focus mode until [your scheduled end time] and will respond to non-urgent requests after that; if you work from home, putting a sign on your door so family members don’t interrupt.

## E – Execute
Big, vague tasks feel overwhelming, so your brain avoids them. This step uses small, focused bursts to turn even the hardest tasks into finished work.
- What to avoid in your answer: Trying to power through a 3-hour task in one sitting without breaks, leading to burnout and procrastination; skipping breaks because you feel like you’re “wasting time”; giving up on a task halfway through because it feels too hard.
- What works better: Using the Pomodoro technique for each A-level work block: set a timer for 25 minutes, work only on your A task with no interruptions until the timer goes off, then take a 5-minute break to stretch, grab water, or walk around; if your A task feels too big (e.g., “write annual report”), break it into tiny 10-minute steps (e.g., “outline report sections,” “gather Q3 data,” “write introduction”) so it’s easy to start; if you finish a Pomodoro early, use the extra time to keep working on the A task before moving to your break to make faster progress.

## R – Review
Without a review, you’ll carry over unfinished tasks forever without fixing what’s broken in your system. This step checks the shape of your work and strengthens your Anvil over time.
- What to avoid in your answer: Forgetting to review your list at the end of the day, so you start the next morning with no clear plan; carrying over unfinished A tasks without adjusting their priority or breaking them down, so they sit on your list indefinitely; beating yourself up for unfinished tasks instead of figuring out why they didn’t get done.
- What works better: Taking 2 minutes at the end of the day to check off every completed task (the physical act gives you a small dopamine hit of accomplishment); if an A task is unfinished, move it to the top of the next day’s A list and break it into smaller steps if it felt too big; if a task has been on your list for 3+ days without progress, ask yourself if it’s actually important (cut it if not) or if you need to break it into smaller, more manageable steps to complete; if you skipped your A tasks, don’t beat yourself up—write down what stopped you (e.g., “coworker interrupted me at 9 a.m.”) and plan a fix for the next day (e.g., “send focus mode message before starting A block”).

Once you’ve filled out every section of this worksheet, you have a complete, stable Anvil for tomorrow. Don’t just file this away—use it. Tomorrow morning, before you check email, before you say yes to any requests, before you scroll social media, start your first A-level work block exactly as you planned. If a request pops up before 10 a.m., ask yourself “Is this more important than the 2-3 A tasks I planned to hit my goals?” If the answer is no, say “I’m booked until 10 a.m. I can look at this after that, or we can schedule it for later in the week.” Protect your A-level time like it’s a meeting with your CEO, because it is: it’s a meeting with the version of you that actually gets what matters done.

If you use this Anvil for just one day, you will get more done before 10 a.m. than you usually get done in a full day. If you use it for 7 days, you will finish every week with 3+ high-priority tasks completed, zero missed critical deadlines, and 2+ hours of free time you didn’t have before. The only thing standing between you and that outcome is filling out this worksheet tonight, and following it tomorrow. Pick up the hammer, place your tasks on the Anvil, and start shaping the results you say you want.
