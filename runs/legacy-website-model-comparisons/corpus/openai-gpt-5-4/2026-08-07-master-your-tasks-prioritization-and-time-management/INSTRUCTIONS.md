# INSTRUCTIONS

## Introduction

Most days do not fall apart because you are lazy. They fall apart because your tasks have no order.

When everything sits on one list with the same visual weight, the loudest item steals your attention. You answer a message, clear a small errand, handle a quick request, and tell yourself you are being productive. But by the end of the day, the work that actually mattered is still unfinished. That is the tension behind this lesson: you can stay busy for hours and still lose the day.

The truth is simple. You do not need a more complicated system. You need a cleaner decision process. The goal of this worksheet is to help you stop reacting and start choosing. If you complete it honestly and specifically, you will know what matters, when you will do it, what must wait, and what needs to be cut.

Your target is not to feel organized. Your target is to complete your top 3 priority tasks on 5 of the next 7 days without working later, checking your phone more often, or adding a new app.

Write like someone who intends to use the page, not admire it. Be specific. Use real tasks. Make visible choices. If an answer is vague, it will not help you when the day gets noisy.

## R — Record everything

Put every task in one place. Do not sort while you capture. Do not decide yet what is important. Just empty your head completely.

Write down work tasks, home tasks, calls, follow-ups, errands, maintenance items, deadlines, things you promised people, and anything you are trying to remember mentally. If it is taking up space in your mind, it belongs on the page.

The point of this section is not elegance. It is full visibility. Your brain is a poor storage system. If you keep tasks in your head, you create pressure before you create order.

Write each task as a clear action, not a vague topic. “Email Sarah the revised numbers” is usable. “Sarah” is not. “Book dentist appointment” is usable. “Health” is not.

Push until the list feels complete enough that you are no longer carrying mental tabs.

What to avoid:
- “Project stuff”
- “Catch up”
- “Work on business”
- Keeping three or four tasks unlisted because you assume you will remember them
- Grouping multiple tasks into one blob like “admin”

What works better:
- “Send contract draft to Marcus”
- “Call insurance company about claim”
- “Buy groceries for Thursday dinner”
- “Review Q3 budget spreadsheet for 30 minutes”
- “Text landlord about leak in kitchen”

## A — Assign value

Mark each task by importance and urgency. Use the worksheet to label every item clearly: A, B, or C. If a task should be delayed, deleted, or delegated, mark that too.

A means it truly matters today. These are the tasks that protect deadlines, create progress, reduce real pressure, or move an important part of life forward.

B means important, but not first. These items matter, but the day will not break if they happen after the A work.

C means minor items, maintenance work, or low-value tasks. These are not fake tasks, but they do not deserve your best hours.

This section requires honesty. Many people sabotage prioritization by making too many things A-level. If everything is critical, nothing is. Force ranking creates clarity. Let the worksheet show what deserves first attention and what does not.

Also identify tasks that should not stay on your plate. Some items need to be delayed, deleted, or delegated. Mark that plainly. A crowded list is often full of unnecessary ownership.

What to avoid:
- Marking half the page as A
- Using emotion instead of value: “I want to do this first because it is easy”
- Keeping low-stakes chores at A level because they are annoying
- Refusing to delegate because “it’s faster if I do it”
- Leaving rollover tasks unexamined just because they have been there a while

What works better:
- A = “Finish proposal due today”
- A = “Call client to resolve issue blocking payment”
- B = “Organize desktop files”
- B = “Research new gym options”
- C = “Clean out old screenshots”
- Delegate = “Ask assistant to schedule service appointment”
- Delay = “Move non-urgent return to Saturday”
- Delete = “Stop rewriting a note no one needs”

## N — Name the big 3

Choose the top 2 to 3 tasks that would make the biggest impact if completed today. These are your big rocks. These are the tasks that make the day count.

Do not choose based on convenience. Choose based on consequence. Ask yourself: if only three things get done today, which three would protect the day, move life forward, or remove the most real pressure?

Write them clearly and specifically. A big 3 task should be concrete enough that you can tell, without debate, whether it was completed.

This part of the worksheet is where the list stops being a pile and becomes a plan. Narrowing matters. Once you choose more than three, you are no longer prioritizing. You are pretending.

If a task feels important but still does not belong in the big 3, leave it as an A or B task. Not everything valuable needs the first slot.

What to avoid:
- Choosing 5, 6, or 7 “top priorities”
- Writing broad categories instead of real outcomes
- Picking easy wins just to feel productive
- Listing tasks that are really multiple projects combined
- Choosing tasks that do not matter much but feel urgent because someone pinged you

What works better:
- “Submit final proposal by 11:00 a.m.”
- “Complete and send invoice batch”
- “Finish outline for presentation due tomorrow”
- “Attend school meeting and confirm next steps”
- “Make hard call to resolve overdue account”

## K — Keep time for what matters

Put your A-tasks, especially your big 3, into your schedule first. Assign real time blocks in your strongest hours. Treat those blocks like actual appointments, not vague intentions.

The worksheet is not asking whether you hope to do the work. It is asking when, exactly, you will do it. Write start times, end times, and the specific task attached to each block.

Place your highest-value work where your energy is best, ideally before messages, inbox cleanup, random errands, and minor admin. If it matters, it must live on the calendar.

Be realistic. Do not stack three hours of fantasy work into one hour of available time. If a task is too large, block the next visible chunk of it. The worksheet should produce a day you can actually execute.

You are not just listing priorities here. You are protecting them with time.

What to avoid:
- “Do proposal sometime this afternoon”
- Scheduling priority work after a long stretch of low-value tasks
- Leaving the whole morning open to interruptions and hoping discipline appears
- Overpacking every hour with no room for transitions
- Blocking time for “work” without naming which task

What works better:
- “8:00–9:30 a.m. — Finish proposal draft”
- “9:45–10:15 a.m. — Client call about payment issue”
- “10:30–11:15 a.m. — Review and send invoice batch”
- “1:00–1:30 p.m. — Return routine emails”
- “3:00–3:30 p.m. — Batch errands and admin calls”

## E — Eliminate switches

Protect the work block by removing distractions and batching similar low-level tasks together. This step turns planned priorities into actual output.

When you write in this section, name what usually breaks your focus. Be specific. Phone checks, open inbox, message alerts, extra tabs, jumping between admin and deep work, random favors, and “quick” side tasks all count.

Then decide what you will do to stop those switches during priority time. Your answer should be behavioral, visible, and enforceable. “Focus better” is useless. “Phone on silent in another room from 8:00 to 9:30” is usable.

Also batch low-value work. Put calls with calls, emails with emails, errands with errands, admin with admin. Do not scatter them through your highest-focus hours.

This section matters because every switch carries a cost. The interruption may take 30 seconds, but the recovery can take far longer. Protecting concentration is part of prioritization.

What to avoid:
- “I’ll try not to get distracted”
- Keeping texts, inbox, and notifications open during deep work
- Mixing serious thinking with routine admin in the same block
- Letting other people’s small requests enter your best hour by default
- Telling yourself one quick check will not matter

What works better:
- “Phone on silent and face down until first A-task block is complete”
- “Inbox closed until 11:30 a.m.”
- “Only one browser tab open for proposal work”
- “Batch all routine calls from 2:00–2:30 p.m.”
- “Write down side thoughts on paper instead of switching tasks”

## D — Decide daily

End the day with a short review. Check what got done, move unfinished tasks forward, and ask why anything stalled.

Use the worksheet to tell the truth about execution. Did your top tasks move? Did you finish them before low-value work took over? If not, do not hide behind vague excuses. Identify the real reason.

Most stalled tasks fall into one of three honest categories:
- it was not actually important,
- it was too big,
- it was unclear.

If it was not important, cut it.
If it was too big, shrink it to the next visible step.
If it was unclear, rewrite it in actionable language.
If it belongs to someone else, delegate it.
If it matters tomorrow, assign it a real slot.

This section keeps your system clean. Without daily decisions, dead tasks linger, guilt builds, and tomorrow starts heavier than necessary. The review does not need to be long, but it must be honest.

What to avoid:
- Rolling over the same task again without changing anything
- Writing “didn’t get to it” instead of naming why
- Keeping nonessential tasks out of guilt
- Treating every unfinished item as tomorrow’s emergency
- Ending the day without resetting the top 3

What works better:
- “Did not finish because I gave 9:00–10:00 to email; tomorrow block proposal first”
- “Task was too big; break into outline, draft, and review”
- “Task is not urgent or important; remove from this week”
- “Need one answer from James before I can continue; send request now”
- “Move unfinished A-task to 8:00 a.m. tomorrow with 45-minute block”

## Finish and act

Complete the worksheet all the way through. Do not stop at the list. A captured list without ranking is stress. Ranking without scheduling is wishful thinking. Scheduling without protection is self-deception. Protection without review breaks down by tomorrow.

Your standard is clear:
- record everything,
- assign value,
- name the big 3,
- keep time for what matters,
- eliminate switches,
- decide daily.

Use what you wrote. Run it for 7 days. Aim to finish your top 3 priority tasks on 5 of those days. Let the page become proof that you can run your day instead of letting the loudest task run you.
